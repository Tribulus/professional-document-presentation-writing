# Professional Document & Presentation Writing

Produce evidence-grounded, usable documents and presentations while preserving the user's intent, requirements, and authorization boundaries.

## Route the task

Recognize intended deliverables even without keywords, e.g. preparing material for a manager briefing. Keep the format open if the user has not chosen one. A follow-up like “shorten slide 6” inherits the current deck's constraints. Reassess them for a new project. Ordinary translation, casual emails and source summaries without a professional deliverable stay outside this workflow.

Infer the lightest mode that can complete the request:

- **Lightweight:** focused rewrite, polish, formatting fix, citation check, or small slide/notes edit with a clear target. Act directly.
- **Exploration:** topic, thesis, audience, or structure is genuinely open. Read the task and supplied material first, then offer 2–4 substantively different directions before drafting.
- **Execution:** the goal and constraints are sufficiently clear. Plan internally as needed and deliver without reopening settled choices.

Inherit constraints already confirmed in the same project or conversation. Do not ask for information already available. Ask only about a missing choice that would materially change direction and cannot be reasonably inferred.

When the output is primarily prose, read [references/document-workflow.md](references/document-workflow.md). When it is a deck, slide outline, or speaker notes, read [references/presentation-workflow.md](references/presentation-workflow.md). For any task using external facts, sources, citations, data, cases, or calculations, also read [references/evidence-and-quality.md](references/evidence-and-quality.md).

For group drafts, cross-section consistency checks, duplicate or term location, or requests for marked-up Word/PPT review copies, follow [Group Work Integration & Consistency Review](references/evidence-and-quality.md#group-work-integration--consistency-review). Route by intent even without those phrases.

## Explore without leading

Clarify progressively in plain language. Let users combine options or say they are unsure. If asked for help deciding, explain your recommendation using available evidence, workload and the rubric. Treat proposed directions as hypotheses until checked. Keep “我的想法” and “其他要求（选填）” separate and optional. Placeholder examples set no defaults. Confirm the research question, main claims, outline and planned evidence together when exploring.

For open-ended work, ground options in the assignment, audience, available material, and evidence feasibility. For each direction give a concrete framing or research question, likely evidence needs, and meaningful tradeoffs. Make options genuinely different rather than cosmetic variants.

Before asking, check whether the options assume an unverified conclusion, leave out a reasonable direction, or steer the user through wording or examples. Use neutral concrete examples to explain how abstract choices differ. Frame hypotheses as open questions. Leave room for another direction or no decision yet. Once direction and outline are confirmed, draft. If the request is already explicit, execute it.

## Shared writing rules

Prioritize accuracy and evidence completeness, then useful reasoning, concision and polish. Delete empty hedging outright. Put material scope inside the factual statement, e.g. “Among the surveyed stores, order volume increased.” Structure academic work around its research question and evidence, and decision documents around the decision, tradeoffs and next actions.

- Preserve the user's intended meaning and voice. Never invent achievements, findings, sources, data, cases, links, or completed work.
- Remove empty padding, repetition, generic throat-clearing, and preventive disclaimers that do not affect the conclusion. Retain conditions that materially change accuracy; consolidate legitimate limitations where readers need them.
- Prefer specific mechanisms, evidence, data, and examples over extra length. Make claims no broader than their support.
- Use public online research when useful. Request access only when a necessary source is restricted. Observe the permissions of the current environment.
- Prefer the user's template, assignment rubric, school rules, and established project conventions over these defaults.
- Keep review notes, assumptions, source gaps, and QA findings separate from the clean final artifact.

## Deliver and verify

Use the artifact-specific tool and format needed for a genuinely usable result. If requested functionality cannot be represented—especially native speaker notes—say so plainly instead of simulating it in body text.

Before delivery, verify the artifact itself, not only the instructions: factual support, citation-to-claim fit, language, logic, calculations, hierarchy, formatting, links, and visual readability. Render or inspect the finished file when layout matters. Report unresolved gaps succinctly and never disguise a partial deliverable as complete.
